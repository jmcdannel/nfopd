How NFOPD calculates

Every snow number in NFOPD comes from public forecasts and snow gauges, run through the same few steps. Here is the short version, then the full method with the formulas, the charts and the test results so far.

Last updated November 27, 2026. The snow formula was tuned on early-season storms and will be re-checked mid-winter. Charts marked "illustrative" use made-up inputs run through the real formulas.

The short version

  • The number is a blend. Each day's snow total is a weighted average of the official National Weather Service (NWS) forecast, in the US, and up to six weather models.
  • It is read high on the mountain. NFOPD picks a point about 85% of the way from base to summit, because a forecast at the base village can miss most of a storm.
  • Liquid becomes snow hour by hour. Each hour's liquid precipitation is multiplied by the share falling as snow and by a snow ratio that tops out at 10 inches of snow per inch of water.
  • No single model runs the show. Sources are weighted from 1 to 3, and when four or more report, the highest and the lowest are left out of the average.
  • Confidence means agreement. The odds and the likely range come from 82 alternate model runs; the confidence score is the share of those runs that land near the middle one.
  • The BS meter checks resort snow reports. It divides the new snow a resort reports by what the nearest automated snow gauge recorded, and treats anything up to 1.3 times the gauge as honest.
  • What is uncertain. The snow formula was tested on about seven weeks of early-season storms, past days on the timeline are model estimates, and no human forecaster reviews any of it.

1 · Forecast point

Where the forecast is read

Resorts are listed at their base village. There, the National Weather Service's 2.5 km forecast cell and the weather models can sit on the valley floor, well below the skiing.

For each North American resort, NFOPD picks an upper-mountain point instead. It checks a grid of spots about 1.1 km apart around the listed location and takes the one whose ground elevation (from SRTM 30 m terrain data) is closest to 85% of the way from base to summit, with a penalty for distance. The search reaches about 5 km.

search radius
  = 4 × vertical drop,
    kept between 1.5 km and 5.5 km,
    plus 1 km
target elevation
  = base + 0.85 × (summit − base)
score
  = |ground elevation − target|
    + 150 ft × distance in km
the lowest score wins

The models are then asked for the summit elevation and the base elevation separately. Open-Meteo, the service that supplies the model data, adjusts temperature for elevation. Each forecast page has a pinpoint map showing the point, and tapping the map moves it.

2 · Sources

What goes in

  • Official forecast. The NWS grid forecast, for US resorts, used as issued.
  • Models in North America, through Open-Meteo: NBM, HRRR (first 2 days), GFS, ECMWF, GEM and ICON.
  • Models in Europe: GFS, ECMWF, ICON, Météo-France, UK Met Office and GEM. In Japan, JMA replaces Météo-France. The Southern Hemisphere adds BOM ACCESS.
  • Ensembles. An ensemble is one model run many times from slightly different starting conditions; each run is a member. NFOPD uses 31 GEFS members and 51 ECMWF members, 82 in all.
3 · The core

Liquid to snow

This is the core of the forecast. NFOPD takes each model's liquid precipitation and converts it to snow hour by hour, at the mountain's elevation, for every model and every ensemble member:

snow = liquid
     × snowFraction(wetBulb)
     × ratio(temperature)

Rain or snow: the snow fraction

Wet-bulb temperature is what a thermometer wrapped in a wet cloth reads. In dry air it runs below the air temperature, which is why snow can fall when the air is a little above freezing. All of an hour's precipitation counts as snow at a wet-bulb temperature of 31°F or colder, none at 34°F or warmer, with a straight line in between. If a model has no wet bulb, air temperature is used and the line runs from 32°F to 35°F.

snowFraction = (34 − wetBulb) ÷ 3
  limited to 0–1, wetBulb in °F

How deep: the snow ratio

The ratio is inches of snow per inch of liquid. NFOPD starts from the Kuchera formula, a standard estimate that makes colder snow fluffier, takes 75% of it and caps the result at 10.

ratio = min(10, 0.75 × kuchera(T))

kuchera(T), T = air temp in kelvins:
  above 271.16 K (28.4°F):
    12 + 2 × (271.16 − T)
  271.16 K and colder:
    12 + (271.16 − T)
  limited to 3–25

Hours are summed by local calendar day. A day counts only if at least 20 of its hours have data.

A worked example

Why not use the models' own snowfall

A model decides rain versus snow at its own smoothed terrain, which is lower than the real mountain, so warm storms come out as rain. NFOPD's older method (daily model snowfall ÷ 7 × Kuchera at the day's maximum temperature) carried that error and was also too generous in cold storms. The two errors partly cancelled.

4 · Test results

How the new method scored against snow gauges

SNOTEL is the US network of automated snow and water gauges in the western mountains. The old and new conversions were compared with 22 SNOTEL gauges from October 9 to November 25, 2026: 153 snow days and 121 storms.

What this test cannot tell you:

  • It is early-season data only. The constants are provisional and will be re-checked mid-winter.
  • SNOTEL measures settled depth, which reads roughly 10–25% lower than a snow-board count.
  • For blended storm totals, the improvement (12%) is not statistically certain.
  • HRRR and forecasts 3–7 days ahead were not tested.
5 · The headline number

The NFOPD blend

For each day, the blend is a weighted average of the sources. The weights are NWS 3, NBM 3, HRRR 2, ECMWF 2, GFS 1, GEM 1 and ICON 1. Abroad: Météo-France 2, JMA 2, UK Met Office 1 and BOM 1.

When four or more sources report, the single highest and the single lowest are left out of the average, so one runaway model can't drive it. They still set the "model range" shown beside the number. NWS is included only for US resorts, at the upper-mountain elevation. The home-page rankings use the same blend without NWS.

blend = sum(weight × snow)
      ÷ sum(weight)
with 4 or more sources, the highest
and the lowest are left out first
6 · Uncertainty

Odds and confidence

These come from the 82 ensemble members' snow totals for days 1–5, 6–10 and 11–15.

  • Likely range: the 10th to the 90th percentile. The 10th percentile is the total that 10% of members fall below.
  • Chance of 1, 6, 12 and 24 inches or more: the share of members at or above that amount.
  • Confidence score, 0 to 100: the share of members within ±40% of the median, the middle member (or within ±1 inch, whichever is larger). High is 70 or more, Medium is 45 or more, otherwise Low.
7 · Measured snow

Last 24 hours and the snow timeline

The last-24-hours number comes from the best available source, in this order: the resort's own report, the nearest SNOTEL gauge (within 45 km, matched for elevation), then the model.

The timeline shows 15 days back, the last 24 hours, and 15 days ahead in 5-day blocks. The days back are model estimates, not measurements. A block's range is the lowest and the highest single-model total.

8 · Accountability

Track record

For resorts near a SNOTEL gauge, NFOPD shows what the models forecast 1, 3 and 5 days ahead over the last 30 days, compared with the water the gauge measured. The comparison is in liquid, so the snow ratio doesn't blur it. A "storm day" is 0.15 inches of water or more.

9 · History

Season so far

Season snowfall uses hourly ERA5/ECMWF reanalysis, a reconstruction of past weather, at summit elevation since October 1 (May 1 in the Southern Hemisphere), run through the same liquid-to-snow conversion. Beside it is the SNOTEL snowpack as a percentage of its 30-year median. It's an estimate.

10 · Resort report check

BS meter

The BS meter compares the new snow a resort reports with what the nearest SNOTEL gauge recorded over the same window: the last 24 hours, 48 hours and 7 days. It covers western US resorts with a gauge within 45 km.

gauge snow = the larger of
  depth gain  and  10 × water gain
ratio = reported snow ÷ gauge snow

Depth gain is the sum of the gauge's day-over-day rises in snow depth. The snowpack settles while it snows, so depth alone undercounts long storms; water doesn't settle. A window counts only when the report or the gauge snow is 2 inches or more, and gauge snow under 1 inch is treated as 1 inch.

The app saves each day's pair of numbers on your device. Until three snow days have been logged, the verdict is shown as an "Early read" from the longest window available, and it says it is a small sample. After three logged snow days it uses the sum over all of them. If the gauge is more than 8 km from the resort, the meter says to treat it as a rough read. It does not score resorts' own forecasts, which NFOPD doesn't read.

11 · The write-up

NFOPD Daily

The regional write-up is AI-generated from NWS forecaster discussions and model numbers. It is not written by a person. It's labelled that way and links its sources.

12 · Timing

Freshness

Single-resort data refreshes about every 30 minutes, and region rankings every 3 hours. If a data provider is rate-limiting, NFOPD shows the last good copy (up to 3 days old) rather than nothing.

13 · Limits

What NFOPD doesn't do

  • There are no human forecasters.
  • Official national forecasts and avalanche bulletins outside the US and Canada aren't read. Those resorts get models only.
  • Past days on the timeline are estimates, not measurements.
  • Forecast points are a terrain-based pick and may sit on a neighboring ridge.

Data: National Weather Service grid forecasts, weather models and ensembles through Open-Meteo, SNOTEL gauges, ERA5/ECMWF reanalysis and SRTM terrain. The charts are drawn in the page from the formulas and numbers shown beside them.
